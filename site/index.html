The week between sessions, kept in your own words.

When something happens, you tell your AI assistant, the way you'd tell a friend. It asks a question or two and writes it down for you. Before your next session, your week is waiting on one page, for you to read, change, and share with your therapist only if you want.

Get started in three steps See what a week looks like

Private by design. Your notes live in a folder on your own computer, not in an app or a cloud.

Therapy gets fifty minutes of your week. The rest of it is easy to lose.

You sit down and remember how you feel right now. Tuesday is a blur. The thing that shook you on Wednesday never quite gets told, or it comes out as “it was a hard week.” sintr helps you keep those moments while they're still fresh, in your own words, so the hour you have is about the week you actually lived.

There's no new app to open and nothing to learn. You talk to the AI assistant you already use, and it keeps the notes for you.

One week, kept.

A fictional week, from Tuesday to Monday. This is what using sintr feels like, from the first message to the session.

Tuesday, 7:40 pm. Something happened at work.

You tell your assistant what happened, the way you'd tell a friend. It listens first. Then it asks one or two questions, like what went through your mind or how strong the feeling was, and writes the moment down in a shape your therapist will recognize. You see it before it's saved.

What happened
When, where, who was there.
What went through your mind
Your exact words, in quotes. Never reworded.
How you felt
The feelings you name, and how strong they were, from 0 to 100.
In your body
Anything you noticed physically.
What you did
What came next. “Nothing” counts.

If you want, it can help you look at a thought more closely. It never pushes, and that can always wait for the session. If your therapist works with CBT, you'll recognize this as a thought record.

Can we write down what happened at planning today? I'm still wound up.

That sounds like a lot landing at once. What happened, and what went through your mind right then?

Three new tasks in the same sprint. I just thought: there's no way I can handle this. And I said yes to all of it.

From 0 to 100, how strong was the anxiety in that moment? Anything in your body?

85. Tight chest, jaw clenched. Still working now.

Thursday, 8:50 am. One line is enough.

A check-in is one message: your mood, your sleep, whatever you feel like saying. No forms to fill in, no streaks, no reminders to feel guilty about.

If you don't mention something, it isn't written down. Nothing is ever made up for you.

Mood 2 out of 5, anxiety 4, slept five and a half hours. Worked late again. 1:1 with my manager today.

Noted for Thursday.

Sunday, 8 pm. Your week, on one page.

On Sunday evening, your assistant puts your week together: what happened, how you felt, what helped, and a few things you might want to bring up. Patterns are written as observations, with dates, and never as a diagnosis. It's a draft for you first. You read it, change anything, and decide whether it goes anywhere.

Resumo

Semana de carga alta no trabalho, com dois picos de ansiedade no início (pressão de prazos em 13/10 e um feedback recebido como crítica em 15/10) e uma recuperação clara no fim de semana, incluindo uma pausa no meio do dia pela primeira vez em semanas e um jantar em família tranquilo.

  • Sono médio 6,7h, com duas noites ruins (6h em 13/10; 5,5h em 15/10), ambas por trabalho à noite.
Situações marcantes

  • 16/10 — Caminhada de vinte minutos sem telefone: culpa 40 e alívio 30; “primeira vez em semanas que parei no meio do dia”.

Temas e padrões recorrentes
  • Observação: as duas situações de maior sofrimento (13 e 15/10) foram no trabalho e envolvendo avaliação/expectativa externa.

Weekly report — Demo Person — 2026-W42

Summary

A week of heavy workload, with two anxiety peaks early on (deadline pressure on Oct 13 and feedback taken as criticism on Oct 15) and a clear recovery over the weekend, including a midday break for the first time in weeks and a calm family dinner.

Mood, energy and sleep
  • Average mood 3.0/5 (lows of 2 on Oct 13 and 15; highs of 4 on Oct 16 and 17).
  • Average anxiety 2.8/5, peaking at 4/5 on Oct 13 and 15 and dropping to 1/5 on Oct 17.
  • Average sleep 6.7h, with two bad nights (6h on Oct 13; 5.5h on Oct 15), both from working late.
Notable situations
  • Oct 13 — Planning meeting with three demands in the same sprint: “there's no way I can handle this”; anxiety 85. Accepted everything on the spot and kept working into the night.
  • Oct 15 — One-on-one feedback from the manager about delivery pace: “he thinks I'm slow”; anxiety 70, shame 55. Apologized repeatedly during the conversation.
  • Oct 16 — Twenty-minute walk without the phone: guilt 40 and relief 30; “first time in weeks I stopped in the middle of the day”.
  • Oct 16 — Calm family dinner: contentment 60; stayed talking until late.
Recurring themes and patterns
  • Observation: the two most distressing situations (Oct 13 and 15) were at work and involved external evaluation or expectations.
  • Observation: in both, the recorded response was to accept or apologize on the spot, without negotiating.
  • Observation: the break on Oct 16 and the weekend improved mood and anxiety (mood 4/5 and anxiety 1/5 on Oct 17).
Topics for the session
  • The pattern of taking on too much on the spot and working at night — what happens just before saying “yes”?
  • Feedback heard as a judgment of personal worth (shame, apologizing repeatedly).
  • The role of sleep in anxiety (sleeping badly → a peak the next day).

Summary prepared from self-reports (check-ins and structured entries) with the help of a personal assistant. Not a clinical assessment.

Nothing leaves without you.

  1. A draft, for you

    Your assistant tells you it's ready, with a short summary.

  2. You read it

    Change any word you like. It's your week.

  3. Your yes

    Only an explicit “send it” sends it. It's the one step that can't be undone, so it never happens on its own.

  4. Sent

    It goes to your therapist the way you chose, like WhatsApp or email, and the summary notes who received it and when.

Monday, 6 pm. You walk in with the week.

The summary doesn't replace the conversation. It gives it a better first five minutes.

Afterwards, tell your assistant what you agreed on. It keeps the homework and brings it back in next week's summary. When something keeps coming up, it points it out gently, as something to explore with your therapist, never as a label.

Your thoughts stay yours.

sintr is a folder on your computer, like a notebook in a drawer. It isn't an app. There's no account and no cloud. Nobody else sees it, and not even your therapist receives anything without your explicit OK.

On your computer
Everything stays in one folder on your own machine. No app, no account, no copy in the cloud.
Locked to you
The folder is set up so other people using the same computer can't open it.
Nothing goes out without your OK
Not even to your therapist. You say yes each time, for that one summary.
You can see what was sent
Every summary records who received it and when.
Never passed around
Your notes are never copied into group chats, shared notes or other services.
Delete means delete
Ask, confirm what exactly, and it's gone. No hidden copies.

One thing to know: sintr runs no servers of its own, but the AI assistant you talk to usually sends what you type to the company behind it, as it does with everything else you tell it. Choose an assistant you're comfortable sharing this with. The technical details

Start in three steps.

You don't need to know anything technical. You need an AI assistant that works directly on your computer and can save files there, such as Claude Code, Hermes, OpenCode or OpenClaw. Assistants that only live in a browser chat can't keep a private folder for you.

Claude Cowork and similar desktop apps need one extra manual step, which is described on the technical page; you may want help from someone technical for it. Not sure about yours? Ask it whether it can save files on your computer.

The setup message

A message for your assistant, not for you. It tells it where to get sintr and how to set it up, and asks it to explain each step in plain words.

Read the message before you copy it
Please set up sintr for me. It's a private journal for therapy that you'll keep for me as files on this computer. Clone https://github.com/tiagomoraes/sintr.git, read docs/INSTALL.md, and run the installer that matches you (./installers/install.sh with hermes, claude-code or generic), using ~/sintr as my journal folder. Explain each step to me in plain words before you do it. Then open ~/sintr/config.md and fill it in with me: ask for my language, time zone and therapy details one at a time, and leave empty anything I don't give you. Keep require_approval: true, and never send anything to anyone without asking me first.

It only uses what's published in the sintr project.

Then just talk to it

Whenever you need to, in your own words. For example:

I need to talk about something that happened today…

Mood 3 out of 5, slept six hours.

Can you put my week together for therapy?

sintr keeps the record. Your therapist does the therapy.

It isn't therapy, it doesn't diagnose, and it isn't for emergencies. It won't label you, judge your moods or tell you what to do. It keeps an honest record of your week, so your time with a professional goes further.

If you tell your assistant you might be in danger, sintr asks it to stop taking notes and help you reach a person right away.

If you're in danger or thinking about harming yourself, please reach a person now.
